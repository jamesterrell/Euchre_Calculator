What this tells you

You are looking at a hand. Somebody has turned a card up. Should you order it? Go alone? Pass and see what happens?

This answers that by playing the hand out thousands of times and keeping score. Not with rules of thumb, and not by asking an expert — it deals the other eighteen cards a few thousand different ways, plays every one of those deals to the last trick, and averages what you scored.

What the number means

Points per deal, to your team. A hand worth +0.9 earns your side about nine tenths of a point every time you take it — sometimes a march for two, often one point, occasionally a euchre against you for minus two. Over a long night it averages out to that.

Anything above zero is a call worth making. The bigger the number the better, and you can compare the three actions directly: they are all on the same scale, from your side of the table.

Why there is a ± after it

Because it is measured, not calculated. Play the same hand a thousand times and you get a slightly different average than the next thousand — the same way your results on a good hand vary over a night of cards. The ± is how much wobble is left. If two actions' ranges overlap, this run cannot tell them apart; drag the slider up and it will play more deals and narrow them.

Going alone is always the wobbliest of the three, because a lone march pays four and a failed one still costs two. It needs roughly four times as many deals to be as sure.

Who you are playing against

Three opponents and a partner who are in exactly your position: they can see their own five cards and the up-card, and nothing else. They think the way this page does — imagine the hands everyone might hold, play them out, pick whatever does best on average. They are good, steady players. They do not signal, they do not have a read on you, and they never make the play that only works because they know what you are holding.

What it does not know

  • The score. It prices every hand as if the game were level. At 9–6 you order things you would never order at 0–0, and this has no idea.
  • Your table. Whether your partner is having a bad night, whether the player on your left always leads trump, whether anyone is counting.
  • Stick the dealer, farmer's hand, and the rest. The house rules are not in here.

So treat it as a strong second opinion about the cards, not as an instruction. It knows the cards better than anyone; it knows nothing else.

The other tab

Solve a whole deal is a different thing. Give it all four hands and it works out exactly what happens if everybody plays perfectly and can see everything — who calls, who takes each trick, what it scores. It is the answer to "what was actually in that hand", which is a good way to settle an argument after the fact. It is not how anybody plays, because nobody can see the other hands.

The slider

More deals, a tighter answer, a longer wait — and the wait is honest: the page tells you roughly how long before you start. A thousand deals takes a few seconds and is usually plenty. Ten thousand is for when two actions are close and you want to know which is really better.

First run after installing, the engine spends a minute or so compiling itself. That happens once.
